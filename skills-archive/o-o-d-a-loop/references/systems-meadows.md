## Player problem or structure problem?

The pre-strategy check, run before strategizing hard against any person: most
recurring "difficult person" situations are structures wearing a face. If the
role would corrupt whoever filled it, the problem is the role. The
highest-yield moves are usually one level up from the players, in the structure
that makes them behave that way - change the structure and different behavior
follows from the same people. Blaming the actor for structure-driven behavior
fixes nothing and burns the relationship that later structural work needs.

## Positional bounded rationality

Every actor behaves rationally given the information that reaches their
position - and that information is late, partial, and distorted. Before
attributing malice or stupidity, sit in the counterpart's node and look at what
they can actually see: what arrives at their desk, on what delay, filtered by
whom, and what they are measured on. Most "irrational" behavior dissolves into
rational response to a bad information position. This is more charitable than
payoff-matrix modeling and more often correct - and it points directly at the
fix, because information flows are a top-tier intervention point.

## Stocks, flows, and feedback

The base vocabulary for "why does this keep happening":

- A **stock** is an accumulation: cash, trust, technical debt, backlog, morale.
- **Flows** fill and drain stocks. A bathtub: the water level (stock) responds
  only to the faucet and the drain (flows).
- **Balancing (negative) feedback** pushes a stock toward a goal - a thermostat.
- **Reinforcing (positive) feedback** compounds - interest, virality, erosion of
  trust that reduces communication that erodes trust further.
- **Delays** separate action from visible effect. Delays in balancing loops
  cause oscillation: overcorrect, overshoot, overcorrect the other way.

Two working corollaries. First, **missing feedback is the most common system
malfunction**: when a situation keeps going wrong, ask what feedback loop is
missing or delayed before redesigning incentives or blaming actors - restoring
an information flow is usually the cheapest powerful intervention. Second,
**stocks respond slowly to changed flows**: a policy change takes time to
accumulate into a visible stock change, so impatience with a lagging stock is
not evidence the intervention failed.

## The twelve leverage points

From Meadows, "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System" (1999), in
increasing order of effectiveness, carried intact:

12. Constants, parameters, numbers (subsidies, taxes, standards)
11. The sizes of buffers and other stabilizing stocks, relative to their flows
10. The structure of material stocks and flows (physical arrangement, plumbing)
9. The lengths of delays, relative to the rate of system change
8. The strength of balancing feedback loops, relative to the impacts they are
   trying to correct against
7. The gain around driving reinforcing feedback loops
6. The structure of information flows - who does and does not have access to
   information
5. The rules of the system (incentives, punishments, constraints)
4. The power to add, change, evolve, or self-organize system structure
3. The goals of the system
2. The mindset or paradigm out of which the system - its goals, structure,
   rules, delays, parameters - arises
1. The power to transcend paradigms - staying unattached in the arena of
   paradigms, holding that no paradigm is true

Her canonical information-flow example (level 6): homes with the electricity
meter visible in the front hall used measurably less power than identical homes
with the meter in the basement. No rule changed, no incentive changed; one
information flow moved.

## Using the list

- **Ask the level question:** at what level is the user intervening, and can
  they move one up? Most people fight at level 12 by reflex - adjusting
  parameters on a structure that regenerates the problem.
- **Check the push direction.** Forrester's observation, which Meadows opens
  with: organizations often know intuitively where their leverage point is - and
  push it hard in the wrong direction. So the question is not only "where is the
  point" but "is the user already pushing it, and which way?"
- **Expect resistance in proportion to leverage.** The higher the intervention
  level, the more the system resists changing it. Parameters are easy and weak;
  goals and paradigms are hard and strong.

## The caution that comes with it

Meadows offered the list tentatively: its order is slithery, every item has
exceptions, and the point is a way of thinking, not a formula. Her closing, kept
in spirit: there are no cheap tickets to mastery - you have to work at it,
whether that means rigorously analyzing a system or rigorously casting off your
own paradigms. The list is an aid to seeing, not a substitute for it.
