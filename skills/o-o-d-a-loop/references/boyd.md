## The diagram

Boyd drew the loop exactly once, in the 1995-96 briefing "The Essence of Winning
and Losing." That single sketch is the canonical reference, and it is not a
circle. The four phases:

- **Observe** - take in raw data: the outside world, the situation, and the
  results of the last action, including the observer's own internal state.
  Observation is not passive; what gets observed is shaped by prior orientation.
- **Orient** - make sense of what was observed. The engine. Boyd drew it bigger
  than the other three combined.
- **Decide** - form a hypothesis. Boyd treated decisions as hypotheses, not
  commitments, because the next Act will test them.
- **Act** - execute, which is also a test. The result feeds straight back into
  Observe and the loop runs again.

What the popular four-box version leaves out:

- **Feedback runs in multiple directions.** Orient feeds Decide, but also feeds
  back to Observe (it tells you what to look for next) and forward to Act
  directly, bypassing Decide.
- **Late commitment.** Because the loop keeps pulling in feedback, you commit as
  late as responsibly possible. This is the structural opposite of
  plan-do-check-act, which locks the plan up front. OODA assumes the situation
  will move under you and builds in agility instead of a fixed plan.
- **It is a system, not a sequence.** A faster loop does not mean racing through
  four steps; it means the cross-connections are tight enough that the whole
  thing behaves like one adaptive instrument.

## Orientation is the center of gravity

Orientation shapes the way we observe, the way we decide, the way we act. Get
Orient wrong and you observe the wrong things, decide on bad data, and act into
a world that is not there. Two people given identical observations reach opposite
conclusions because their Orient is built differently.

Boyd broke Orient into five interacting inputs: cultural traditions, genetic
heritage, previous experience, new information, and analysis-synthesis. Only the
last is directly operable at decision time; the others are stocks built earlier.
The practical consequence: the quality of a decision session is mostly the
quality of the orientation work done inside it, and the quality of a fast
in-the-moment call is mostly the quality of orientation built before it.

## Implicit guidance and control

In Boyd's diagram, arrows run from Orient straight to Act and from Orient straight
back to Observe, bypassing Decide entirely. He called these implicit guidance and
control. When orientation is good enough that a situation matches a pattern
already owned, there is no deliberation; the decision was effectively pre-made by
the quality of the orientation. Boyd's word for the feel of this was
Fingerspitzengefuehl - fingertip feel.

This is not recklessness. The line between "reckless" and "implicit guidance" is
whether the orientation underneath was actually built: stable environment, deep
practice, fast unambiguous feedback (see the Kahneman-Klein conditions in
`bets-and-behavior.md`). Preparation is how implicit guidance gets engineered:
front-load complexity into prep so execution stays clean. The prepared operator
runs Orient-to-Act while the unprepared one cycles through Decide on every step.

## Destruction and creation

Boyd's one published essay (1976) is the epistemology under the Orient node. The
argument, built from Goedel's incompleteness, Heisenberg's uncertainty, and the
second law of thermodynamics: any closed mental model degrades over time and
eventually fails to match reality. To stay accurate you must periodically destroy
the model and build a new one.

- **Destruction (analysis):** break the current concept into parts. Take apart
  what you believe about the situation.
- **Creation (synthesis):** recombine parts - including parts pulled from
  unrelated domains - into a new whole that did not exist before. Boyd's stock
  example: boards off skis, motor off a boat, handlebars off a bicycle, tread off
  a tractor; recombined, a snowmobile. None of the sources predicted it.

Boyd's blunt rule: "You have to learn how to unlearn. People who can't unlearn,
we call them dinosaurs." Survival is the capacity to reject an outdated model and
replace it, not the ability to defend the one you have. Session tell: a model
that has survived unchanged through several rounds of new information is being
defended, not updated.

## Tempo and fast transients

Boyd's strategic payload: operate your loop faster than the opposing process and
you generate fast transients - changes too rapid for the opponent to track. They
end up reacting to a situation that no longer exists, which produces confusion,
then paralysis. He called this getting inside the adversary's OODA loop. The
opponent is often not a person; it is entropy, the clock, and stalled
deliberation.

## Loop-speed matching

The speed advantage applies in genuinely adversarial, fast-transient conditions:
live operations, a production incident, a negotiation in motion. It is exactly
wrong for high-stakes, low-reversibility decisions made under emotional
activation, which punish speed and reward the explicit Decide step. Speed
amplifies whatever the orientation is; a fast loop on a distorted Orient executes
errors at speed, with total conviction.

So the operating rule is not "go fast." It is **match loop speed to the
engagement**: fast implicit loop for prepared, reversible, time-critical work;
slow explicit loop for irreversible, high-stakes, emotionally loaded decisions.
Knowing which mode applies is itself an Orient function.

## Common misreadings

- **"Speed of iteration beats quality of iteration."** Attributed to Boyd; he
  never said it. Orient is the largest node because iteration quality lives
  there. Velocity for its own sake is faster waste.
- **The four-box circle.** Drops the multi-directional feedback, the late
  commitment, and implicit guidance - the parts that carry the content.
- **OODA as pure competition.** The loop is a tempo-and-adaptation framework, not
  a payoff framework; it applies unchanged to non-adversarial situations where
  the opponent is entropy or one's own indecision.
