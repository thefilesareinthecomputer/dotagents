## Is there a game at all?

The first and cheapest question when another party appears in a decision. A game
requires another mind optimizing its own payoffs. Most "opponents" are nature
wearing a face: entropy, the clock, randomness, an institution's inertia. The
manufactured-opponent error - treating dice as a schemer - imports adversarial
criteria (worst-case flooring, secrecy, bluffing) into situations that reward
plain expected-value reasoning. The reverse error, treating a genuine adversary
as nature, loses to anyone modeling you back. Route first, then reason.

Game theory's jurisdiction begins only after this question answers yes. It is
prescriptive after the payoff structure is fixed; when the situation is
ambiguous, evolving, or cooperative, tempo and adaptation (the loop itself)
matter more than equilibrium analysis.

## Modeling the other mind

Model **revealed payoffs, not stated ones**: what the counterpart's actions and
incurred costs say they value, not what they announce. Talk is cheap; costly
action is evidence.

Second question: **what is hidden?** Hidden information (they know something you
do not) and hidden action (they can do things you cannot observe) are the two
standard asymmetries. Many "irrational" moves become rational once the hidden
variable is guessed; hunt for it before concluding the counterpart is a fool.

Before attributing malice or stupidity at all, apply the positional check from
`systems-meadows.md`: every actor behaves rationally given the information that
actually reaches their position, and that information is late, partial, and
distorted. Sitting in the counterpart's node and looking at what they can see is
more charitable than payoff-matrix modeling and more often correct.

## Repeated games

One-shot logic and repeated-game logic invert. In a one-shot interaction,
defection can dominate; under repetition with memory, cooperation sustained by
reciprocity wins (Axelrod). The strategies that endure are nice (never defect
first), retaliatory (respond to defection promptly), forgiving (return to
cooperation after punishment), and clear (legible enough to be modeled).

Session question: is this counterpart a one-shot stranger or a repeated-game
partner? Most professional relationships are repeated games being misplayed with
one-shot logic. The shadow of the future - how likely and how valuable future
rounds are - sets how much cooperation the present round can bear.

## Mechanism design: reverse game theory

Game theory takes the rules as given and solves for play. Mechanism design picks
the desired outcome and derives the rules that make self-interested players
produce it. The routing question: **which rules of this game do you actually
control?** The agenda, the format, the default, the information flows, the
incentives, who moves first, what gets measured. If any of those are yours, fix
the structure so the desired behavior or the truth emerges on its own, rather
than out-playing the participants round after round.

Self-application: anyone who designs their own systems is both designer and
player, and the player will game the designer. Write rules where gaming them and
doing the right thing are the same act. This is why precommitment devices work
where willpower plans fail: the device changes the game the future self plays,
instead of hoping the future self plays better.

The field's central caution (the revelation principle's practical shadow):
mechanisms only work when incentive-compatible - when telling the truth or doing
the right thing is each participant's best move under the rules as written, not
as intended.

A convergence worth trusting: Meadows reached "change who sees what
information" as a top-tier intervention from ecology (see
`systems-meadows.md`, level 6); Hurwicz reached the same result from
economics - mechanism design's informational efficiency. Independent
derivation from two unrelated fields is the strongest evidence a joint
between frameworks is real.

## Goodhart's law and the scheduled review

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Every mechanism
gets gamed eventually, including self-pointed ones; every metric drifts from the
goal it once proxied. The counter is structural, not moral: schedule a review
that can revise the mechanism itself. A metric without a scheduled audit of the
metric is a future pathology. (This is the systems lens's top intervention level
applied to mechanisms: keep the power to change the rules about the rules.)

## Ostrom: mechanism design found in the wild

Ostrom documented communities governing commons successfully for centuries
without markets or central states. Her recurring design principles are mechanism
design discovered empirically: clear boundaries on who participates; rules
matched to local conditions; the governed participate in writing the rules;
monitoring by parties accountable to the governed; graduated sanctions rather
than one-strike ruin; cheap, fast conflict resolution; recognized authority to
self-organize. When abstract mechanism theory and Ostrom's field evidence
disagree, bet on Ostrom.
