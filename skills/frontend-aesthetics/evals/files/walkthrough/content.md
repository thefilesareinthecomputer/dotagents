# Tamber pocket field spectrometer - walkthrough page content

Evaluation fixture. Tamber, Tamber Instruments, and every specification below are invented; nothing here describes a real product.

## Page purpose

A product walkthrough for people who work outdoors with plants, soil, pigments, and minerals: field ecologists, conservators, growers, and geology students. The page walks a visitor through one complete reading, from aiming the device to logging the result. It is not a specification sheet and it has no pricing.

## Hero

Headline: See what light knows.

Supporting line: Tamber is a pocket spectrometer that reads the color of anything you point it at, down to the wavelength, and tells you what that color means.

Primary action: Follow a reading (jumps to step 1)

Secondary action: Read the field guide (links to `#field-guide` at the bottom of the page)

## The five steps

### Step 1 - Aim

Hold Tamber two to three centimeters from the sample. The ring light around the aperture wakes when it senses something close, so you never press a button just to see where you are pointing.

Image description: a palm-sized oval device in dark slate held over a single leaf, with a thin ring of warm white light at the aperture casting a small bright circle on the leaf.

### Step 2 - Capture

Press the side button once. Tamber takes a 0.4 second scan across 340 to 1000 nanometers, from near ultraviolet to near infrared, and repeats it three times to average out a shaky hand.

Image description: the same device from the side, showing the single raised side button, with three short pulses of light drawn as concentric arcs leaving the aperture.

### Step 3 - Watch the light split

The reading arrives on your phone as a thin white line. The line fans out into its colors, violet on one side and deep red on the other, and then the top edge of that band settles into a curve: how much light the sample reflected at every wavelength.

Image description: a single thin white horizontal line that widens into a band running violet, blue, green, yellow, orange, red, left to right; the upper edge of the band becomes a smooth curve with a low point in the blue and a steep rise past the red.

### Step 4 - Match

Tamber compares the curve against a library of 2,400 reference samples and shows the three closest matches. Each match is labeled Strong, Possible, or Weak, never as a percentage, because a percentage suggests more certainty than a field reading has.

Image description: three result rows, each with a small square color swatch, a sample name (Healthy oak leaf, Drought-stressed oak leaf, Iron oxide pigment), and a one-word match label.

### Step 5 - Log

Save the reading to your field notebook with the time, your note, and your phone's location if you allow it. Export any day's readings as a CSV file for your own analysis.

Image description: a notebook page with three timestamped entries, each with a small curve thumbnail and a short handwritten-style note.

## The moment to remember

Step 3 is the moment the page should be remembered for: the thin white line splitting into its colors and settling into a curve. It is the one place the page spends its boldness. Everything else supports it.

A visitor who uses reduced motion must still get this moment as a finished, still image: the full spectrum band with its curve, labeled, with the same explanatory text.

## Specifications (invented)

- Range: 340 to 1000 nm
- Scan time: 0.4 seconds, averaged over three scans
- Reference library: 2,400 samples, updated with the companion app
- Battery: about 900 readings per charge
- Weight: 68 g
- Connection: Bluetooth to the companion app on a phone

## Field guide

Section id: `field-guide`

Heading: Before your first reading

Text: Calibrate once a day against the white tile in the case lid. Keep the aperture clean; a fingerprint shows up as a dip near 450 nm. Readings through glass or plastic are not reliable.
